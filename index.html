Research Note № 1

Ha Jin — Who He Is & Why This Topic

Sources — Los Angeles Review of Books (2015) · Literary Hub (2019) · Poetry Foundation
— Born Jin Xuefei in northern China, 1956 — Lived through the Cultural Revolution as a child/teenager — Was a member of the Red Guard, then a SOLDIER in the PLA (People's Liberation Army) for 6 years → This is why the military hospital setting in Waiting is so specific and accurate. He was there. — When the Cultural Revolution ended and universities reopened, Jin studied English — Immigrated to the US in 1986 → Brandeis University, American literature — STAYED in the US after watching Tiananmen Square massacre on TV in 1989 Did not go back. — Has said he writes in English to "separate my existence from the state power of China and to preserve the integrity of my work." (Literary Hub, 2019) — Most of his novels are BANNED in China. Waiting was published there but with cuts — "They cut paragraphs and they cut sentences. Whenever a piece of my writing gets reprinted, you can see more cuts." — Ha Jin — He is personally banned from traveling to China.
Connection to Waiting

Lin Kong is a soldier-doctor caught between two worlds — exactly Ha Jin's own experience. The repression Lin feels from the state is not invented. Jin lived it. The 18-year divorce law, the army rules about relationships, the shame in the divorce court — all drawn from a real system Jin witnessed firsthand. Writing Waiting in English was itself an act of resistance against the same state power the novel is about.

"Under the thumb of the state, so much imagination is wasted."
— Literary Hub, 2024
Research Note № 2 — History & Data

Stanford SPICE — CCP Control of Daily Life

Source — Stanford University SPICE (Stanford Program on International and Cross-cultural Education)
TWO PRIMARY METHODS OF CONTROL: 1. Assigning CLASS LABELS to every person 2. Giving the boss/leader of each work unit (danwei) nearly unlimited control over workers' lives Work unit bosses controlled: → Housing → Food rations → Marriage approvals → Travel permits → Job assignments Freedom of expression: completely denied. People were TOTALLY dependent on bosses, had to remain obedient to the state or face: — loss of food/housing — public shame — imprisonment — forced labor Educated workers (doctors, teachers, scholars) were especially vulnerable — labeled "class enemies" or "rightists" during the Cultural Revolution. Personal decisions like WHO YOU MARRY were not truly personal — they required work unit approval.
Connection to Waiting

This explains everything about why Lin cannot simply divorce Shuyu and marry Manna. It is not just a personal choice — it has to go through the army's approval process. The commissar, the divorce court, the public shame — all expressions of the danwei system. Lin is one of these educated workers (a doctor) whose personal life is controlled at every level by the institution he works for. The judge calling Lin a "revolutionary officer" who has forgotten his class origin is not dramatic — it is exactly what this system did.

Research Note № 3 — History & Data

Constituting America — Cultural Revolution Overview

Source — Constituting America, Cultural Revolution essay
— Cultural Revolution lasted 1966–1976 → EXACT time period Waiting is set in — Mao launched it to purge "capitalist roaders" and re-energize communist ideology A general purge of ALL freedoms: → Speech → Private property → Education → Independent thinking Red Guards (youth militias) enforced ideology: — humiliated, beat, and killed perceived enemies "Many people were sent to reeducation camps far removed from the eyes of people in larger cities, at which hundreds of thousands — possibly millions — were tortured or killed." Working class of educated people specifically targeted: — doctors, teachers, scholars — forced to work in mass food plantations Regime controlled: media, personal belongings, even home decorations. Marriage and family life: divorce heavily restricted. Relationships had to conform to revolutionary ideals. DEATH TOLL: estimates range from 500,000 to 2 million+ Millions more imprisoned or "sent down" to labor camps.
Connection to Waiting

Lin is a doctor — exactly the kind of educated professional targeted by the Cultural Revolution. His restricted ability to divorce is not a small bureaucratic inconvenience; it is the direct product of a system that imprisoned and killed millions for less. The "abyss of misfortune" the judge warns Lin about is not metaphorical. It is real. Waiting takes place inside one of the most controlled societies in modern history, and Jin never lets you forget it.

Research Note № 4 — Documentary

Morning Sun (2003)

Directors — Carma Hinton & Richard Gordon · Available on Kanopy (free with library card)
FULL TITLE: Morning Sun (2003) DIRECTORS: Carma Hinton & Richard Gordon LENGTH: ~2 hours WHERE TO WATCH: Kanopy (free with library card), YouTube clips WHAT IT'S ABOUT: — "Attempts, in the space of two hours, to create an inner history of the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution (c.1964–1976)." — Kanopy description — Multi-perspective: told through eyes of the high school generation born around the founding of the PRC in 1949 — the generation that CAME OF AGE in the 1960s during the Revolution — Uses archival footage, propaganda films, Red Guard music, and personal interviews — Focuses on the PSYCHOLOGICAL experience: how people genuinely believed in Mao, and how that belief collapsed KEY DETAILS: — Shows how young people were REQUIRED to report on their own families if they said anything against Mao → the state colonizing the most intimate relationships — Covers the period when divorce would have been nearly impossible to obtain without political consequences → exactly Lin's situation — The generational angle: Lin is of the generation the documentary focuses on. He is not unusual. He is REPRESENTATIVE. — Shows "sending down" — youth sent to rural labor camps, cut off from family and education
Connection to Waiting

Morning Sun shows the faces and voices of people who lived exactly what Ha Jin wrote about. Where Waiting gives you one man's quiet experience of the system, Morning Sun shows the system itself — the rallies, the propaganda, the mass psychology. Together, they show both sides: the machinery of oppression AND the individual life crushed inside it. Lin Kong could have been one of the interview subjects in this film.

Research Note № 5 — Book Commentary

Literary Hub — "Timeless and Urgent: On Ha Jin's Waiting" (2024)

Source — Literary Hub, September 2024 · lithub.com/timeless-and-urgent-on-ha-jins-waiting
AUTHOR'S CENTRAL ARGUMENT: — Waiting is about "the arbitrary demands and strictures placed on individuals" by the authoritarian state — Key idea (paraphrased): under the thumb of the state, so much imagination is wasted — the question the novel raises is: how could these people's lives have been different if they hadn't been subjected to authoritarian rule? IMPORTANT DETAIL — MANNA'S BODY IS ALSO STATE PROPERTY: — Nurses had their virginity verified by physical exam before being allowed to join the army hospital — "Every young woman recruited had to go through a physical exam that eliminated those with a broken hymen." — In 1964, binding feet is old-fashioned, but this invasive checking of hymens is "accepted as standard practice" — Manna is NOT free either — she is also controlled by patriarchal, Confucian beliefs, and the authoritarian state ON THE VALUE OF WORLD LITERATURE: — Waiting is described as "an imaginative exercise" that makes visible what news articles and data cannot: the texture of a life lived under authoritarianism, year by year, wait by wait — Fiction gives SHAPE to historical experience that statistics and history books cannot
Connection to the Assignment's Big Question

This article directly answers "what is the value of world literature?" A number like "2 million deaths" tells you nothing about what it feels like to wait 18 years for a divorce while your life drains away. Waiting does. That is the unique power of world literature — and this commentary names it explicitly.

Research Note № 6 — Other Artistic Medium

To Live (活着) — dir. Zhang Yimou (1994)

Sources — Deep Focus Review · Film Sufi · Letterboxd · Spirituality & Practice · Wikipedia
ORIGINAL TITLE: 活着 (Huózhe — "To Live") DIRECTOR: Zhang Yimou YEAR: 1994 BASED ON: Novel by Yu Hua (1993) STARS: Ge You, Gong Li WHAT IT'S ABOUT: — Follows a married couple, Fugui and Jiazhen, from the 1940s through the Chinese Civil War, the Great Leap Forward, and the Cultural Revolution — Spans 4 decades — every time the family starts to stabilize, the political system destroys something again — "The destinies of individuals are determined by forces beyond their control." — Spirituality & Practice KEY SCENE — THE BURNING PUPPETS: — Fugui is forced to BURN his shadow puppets (his livelihood and identity) when it becomes politically dangerous to own anything associated with pre-Communist culture — Zhang Yimou lingers on the burning puppets: a scene about the destruction of personal identity by the state — This directly mirrors Lin destroying his personal life to conform to army rules BANNED IN CHINA: — China allowed Zhang to film it because it showed "the resilience of the Chinese people" — China BANNED it after release because it critiqued the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution — Both readings are correct — that is what makes it great CRITICAL RECEPTION: — "A portrait of resilience and a biting critique — Zhang doesn't tip the scales in one direction." — Letterboxd
Comparison to Waiting

Both works follow ordinary people — not heroes, not dissidents — just people trying to live under an impossible system. Both refuse easy resolution: in To Live, survival itself is the only victory. In Waiting, Lin gets what he waited for, and it means nothing. Zhang Yimou and Ha Jin make the same argument through different mediums: authoritarianism doesn't just kill people. It hollows out their lives slowly, through accumulated surrender and deferred choices. A history book tells you the Cultural Revolution lasted 10 years and killed 2 million. To Live shows you a father watching his son die in a senseless accident caused by political chaos. Waiting shows you a man who waited so long for love that he forgot how to feel it. These are truths that data cannot hold.

"Waiting is a novel about accumulating surrenders, evasions, and eventually, the weight of a life that is half-lived."
— Joshua Lam, letter to Ha Jin
